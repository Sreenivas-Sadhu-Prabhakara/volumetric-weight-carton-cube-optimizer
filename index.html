How it works

A carrier bills the greater of two numbers. One comes off the weighing scale. The other comes off the tape measure: multiply the outside length, width and height of the carton, divide by a number the carrier chooses, and call the answer kilograms. That number is the volumetric divisor, and it is the whole game. A courier card dividing by 5,000 is saying that anything lighter than 200 kg per cubic metre will be billed on its size rather than its weight. An air-freight card dividing by 6,000 says 167 kg per cubic metre. A surface card dividing by 4,000 says 250. The same carton, unchanged, can be billed at three different weights on the same day.

So the page computes both weights and says plainly which one is charging you and by how much. volumetric kg = L × W × H ÷ divisor, then chargeable = the greater of actual and volumetric, rounded up to the carrier's step. The gap between the two is the air you are paying for, and it is priced twice over: once as the extra kilograms, and once as money, by pricing the shipment again with the volume rule switched off and taking the difference.

The two weights cross at exactly one volume: break-even volume = actual weight × divisor. Below it the scale is billing you and the extra size is free; above it the tape measure is billing you and the extra weight is free. From that one line come the tipping height — hold the length and width, and this is the height at which the box flips over — and the same box scaled onto the line, which is the largest box of that shape that would still be billed on the scale.

Sea LCL looks like a different rule and is not. It is billed per revenue tonne: the greater of the cubic metres and the metric tonnes, the weight-or-measurement rule, at 1 CBM to 1,000 kg. That is the same comparison with a divisor of 1,000, so the page runs the same arithmetic and changes only the billing unit and the one-revenue-tonne minimum.

Units per carton, cartons per pallet

The fit side is deliberately simple arithmetic, and it is important to say what it is not. The page takes the unit, takes the carton's inside dimensions — the outside, less two wall thicknesses on each axis — and lays the units out in a plain grid: so many along, so many across, so many layers. It does that in all six ways the unit can be turned, and reports the best, with the cube fill and the void left over. Pallets and containers are the same routine with the carton as the item.

This is an axis-aligned orientation check, not a bin-packing solver. Every unit lies the same way up in a regular grid. Real packers beat it: mixed orientations, interlocked and pinwheel layers, nesting, a bit of squeeze on a soft product. The gap is not small — a 20-foot container takes eleven 1200 × 800 pallets on a mixed floor pattern where this grid check finds eight. Read every count on the page as a safe floor you can certainly achieve, never as an optimum, and never as a reason to tell a packer they are doing it wrong.

The void matters because it is not free. Empty space inside a carton weighs nothing on the scale and plenty on the tape measure, so the page prices it: the void volume run through the same divisor is the weight you are paying to ship air. That is what links the two halves. The last table takes the units you are actually putting in the carton, finds the smallest grid box that holds them, and prices that box against the one you are using. When the smaller box drops the parcel back onto its actual weight, the saving is the whole difference.

Assumptions & rates
    • Chargeable weight is compared per carton. All cartons in a shipment are treated as identical, which is why the per-carton and whole-shipment comparisons agree. A mixed shipment has to be checked carton by carton, and some carriers compare the summed volume of the whole shipment against the summed scale weight instead.
    • Rounding applies to the chargeable weight, in your own mass unit, after the comparison — —. Sea LCL bills revenue tonnes to three decimals, so the kilogram step is not applied in that mode.
    • Some carriers round each dimension up to the next whole centimetre before multiplying. This page uses the figures you type. If yours does, enter the rounded-up dimensions.
    • Carriers measure the outside of the box at its widest point: tape, bulge, protruding labels and all. Measure the carton as it leaves the packing bench, not as it reads on the flat.
    • Carton inside dimensions are the outside less two wall thicknesses per axis, from the board thickness you enter. The single-wall default is —; double-wall board runs roughly 6 to 8 mm.
    • Cube fill and the void are measured against the units actually going in the carton, not against its capacity, because a half-filled carton is charged on the same cube as a full one.
    • Switching between centimetres and inches converts every field you have typed and rewrites it rounded for legibility — to one decimal in centimetres, two in inches. The divisor is converted the same way, so a custom divisor survives the switch instead of jumping back to the mode default. A rate per pound is finer than a rate per kilogram, and a half-pound rounding step is finer than a half-kilogram one, so the two systems can differ by a fraction of a percent. That is the rate card's doing, not the arithmetic's.
    • The fit grid assumes no clearance between units and no dunnage or void fill. In practice allow a few millimetres per unit, and remember a carton maker works to the sizes and flutes they stock, so round a right-size box up to the nearest one they quote.
    • The right-size box keeps the empty-carton weight you entered. A smaller box uses less board and weighs less, so the saving shown is understated rather than overstated.
    • Pallet footprints are —, all ISO 6780 sizes, in millimetres. Overhang is not allowed for and the pallet deck height is subtracted from your stack limit.
    • Container internal dimensions are —. They are indicative: they differ by builder and by age of the box, sometimes by several centimetres, so take them from the container you are actually given. The door opening is always smaller than the inside, and a loaded pallet that clears the inside height but not the door does not go in — the page checks that.
    • Pallets on a container floor are a single tier in one orientation. Double-stacking is often possible if the cargo bears the load, and a mixed floor pattern fits more.
    • Freight rates open with a round indicative placeholder so the money column is never blank: —. They are illustration, not quotations. Fuel, security, remote-area, residential and handling surcharges, demurrage, duties and taxes are all out of scope.
    An estimating tool for the packing bench, not a quotation and not a carrier's invoice. Divisors, rounding steps, minimum chargeable weights and surcharges are set by each carrier in each contract, and the only authority on what you will be billed is your own rate card. The fit counts are a conservative grid check, not a packing optimum. Nothing you type leaves your device.
